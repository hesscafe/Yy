# Monopoly lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

Hess Cafe is a morning café in a dense coffee market, and so far it has nothing that would stop a competitor. Its one advantage is structural: it runs inside a bar that sits empty in the morning, pays rent only as a share of revenue and has a $2,000 start-up cost. That keeps the downside small. It does not protect the business. The way to make it hold is to dominate one small market first: the West Village customer who wants a dessert-flavoured latte worth a photo.

## What is strong
- **Low fixed cost.** Rent is 25–30% of revenue with no utilities, so a slow month costs little and the founder risks only about $2,000. The brief does not show that a rival café with a normal lease could match this cost structure.
- **A small market it could own.** Dessert-inspired drinks (cherry pie matcha, halva latte, apple strudel latte, hot chocolate with torched meringue) are a sharper niche than "coffee in the West Village." The niche is narrow enough that one shop could plausibly become the place people think of for it.
- **A possible start on distribution.** The drinks are made to be photographed, and the brief expects Instagram-driven weekend visitors. If that works, each customer becomes free reach. So far it is an assumption, not something shown.
- **A model that can be copied.** Running a morning café inside a bar is a format that could later move into other bars that sit idle in the morning. That is the "expand from a small market" path, if the first site works.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No hold on the location.** The whole business sits inside someone else's space, and the term, notice period, exclusivity and morning signage are all unknown. If the bar can end the deal, bring in a different morning operator, or copy the menu once it is proven, the founder has built value for the host. The brief gives nothing the bar could not take back.
2. **Easy to copy and no secret.** Flavoured matcha and dessert lattes are recipes, not proprietary know-how, and any nearby café could add a "cherry pie matcha" within weeks. Nothing in the brief is roughly 10x better than the alternatives or durable over ten years. Without a brand that becomes the destination, by month six the drinks are just items on other cafés' menus.
3. **Competition and visibility in a crowded market.** The West Village has many coffee shops (the brief names none, which is an open question). A café hidden inside a bar, with removable décor and possibly no outdoor morning signage, has to win commuters, who buy out of habit and convenience, against established shops they already walk past. Distribution to weekday buyers is unproven.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- **Agreement:** What is the term, the notice period and the exclusivity? Can the bar run its own morning coffee or use your recipes if you leave? Can you put signage outside in the morning?
- **Competition:** Which cafés within a 5-minute walk serve the same commuter, and does any of them already sell flavoured matcha or dessert lattes?
- **The secret:** What do you know that others do not? Is it your recipes, an audience you already have, or this particular empty-in-the-morning slot?
- **First 100 customers:** How do they arrive, specifically, and what does each one cost? Do you already have a social following, or local or creator relationships?
- **Daily volume:** How many customers a day do you expect on weekdays and on weekends, and what does each drink cost to make? Without these, there is no way to check the business is viable at a 25–30% revenue share.
- **The team:** Do you work every shift alone? What happens when you are sick?
- **Why now:** Why is this the right moment for dessert matcha in the West Village, rather than a trend that peaks and fades?
- **Durability:** What does this look like in year three? Is it one site, more bars, or a packaged product?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **A signed agreement** with a minimum term (12 months or more), a written notice period, exclusivity for the morning coffee slot, a clause saying the founder owns the recipes and brand, and permission for morning signage outside.
2. **Licences confirmed.** A clear answer that the morning café is covered by a permit and passes health inspection, and who carries the liability. Without this, there is no business to protect.
3. **A distribution test before spending on décor.** Pick one specific way to reach the first 100 customers and measure what each one costs over 2–4 weeks: an Instagram launch, a creator visit or a commuter offer. Weekend Instagram traffic only counts once it actually shows up.
4. **A plan to build a brand.** Pick one signature drink to be known for, so the brand, rather than the recipe list, is what keeps customers coming.
