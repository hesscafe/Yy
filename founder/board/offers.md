# Offers lens
Score: 5/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **Downside is small.** About $2,000 to start, no fixed rent (25–30% revenue share) and no utilities. If it fails, little is lost, so it is a cheap test of real demand.
- **The market has money and can be reached.** West Village locals and commuters already buy coffee every day, and the weekend visitors who come for Instagram are already looking for something to post. Both groups walk past 70 Christopher St.
- **The drinks are not a commodity.** A strawberry cheesecake matcha, a cherry pie matcha, a halva latte, an apple strudel latte and hot chocolate under torched meringue give people a reason to come that is not "cheaper coffee". The $7–8 price is not a discount play, which is the right instinct.
- **The dream outcome is clear for the weekend buyer:** a drink nobody else has, which looks good on camera, in a fun space. A drink delivers that result in minutes, so the time score is high.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Nothing gives the weekday commuter a reason to choose it.** On the value equation the commuter's dream outcome is good coffee, fast, on the way. The brief says nothing about speed, quality or the regular coffee menu (prices are unknown), and it offers no food. On weekdays this is a commodity competing with every coffee shop on the route (the competitors are unknown). If weekdays don't pay, weekend volume alone has to carry rent, stock and the founder's time.
2. **Buyers can't trust what they can't see.** The café lives inside a bar that is closed in the morning, with a design that has to be removable and no confirmed outdoor signage. A passer-by may not know it exists or believe it is open, which drags the "perceived likelihood" score toward zero. With no proof, no reviews and no visible storefront, the offer barely registers.
3. **The margin may not support the price.** The revenue share takes 25–30% off the top, and the signature drinks use specialty inputs (matcha, flavour syrups, meringue that has to be torched to order). The cost per drink is unknown. The equipment is unknown, and $2,000 does not buy a commercial espresso machine and grinder if the bar has none. Torching meringue to order also slows service, which hurts the rush hour.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What does one signature drink cost you to make, and what is left from $7–8 after the 25–30% share?
- How many drinks a day do you need to break even and pay yourself, and how many do you expect on weekdays versus weekends?
- Why would a commuter pick you over the coffee they already buy? What makes you faster, better or easier?
- What do the regular coffee and the hot chocolate cost, and can the signature drinks carry a higher price (could you double it and still be the obvious choice)?
- How does someone walking down Christopher St at 7am know you are open? Do the bar's terms allow signage outside in the morning?
- What does the bar already have that you may use (espresso machine, grinder, fridge, dishwasher), and what is left to buy from $2,000?
- Do the bar's licences cover a morning café, and who is liable?
- What are the term, notice period and exclusivity in the deal with the bar? Could they end it once you have built the audience?
- Is there a stack beyond single drinks, such as a weekday punch card or subscription, a pre-order for pickup, a pastry pairing or a weekend limited drink?
- What is the guarantee? For example, "If you don't love your signature drink, we remake it free."

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **The numbers close on paper.** Know the cost per drink for each signature item and the regular coffee, and show that the drinks needed a day to break even (after the 25–30% share) are believable for a 6-hour morning.
2. **The deal and permits are confirmed in writing:** the equipment you may use, licence and permit cover for morning food service, who carries liability, a minimum term with a notice period, and the right to put signage outside in the morning.
3. **A reason to come on weekdays exists.** Add one concrete thing for the commuter, such as speed (pre-order or a set grab-and-go menu), a punch card or subscription, or a simple food add-on. Weekends cannot carry the business alone.
4. **Proof before scale.** Run a 2–4 week test with real prices before spending the décor budget, and track drinks a day by weekday versus weekend and the mix of signature versus regular drinks.
5. **The offer has a name, a stack and a guarantee:** a rotating limited "drink of the week" for real urgency, a remake guarantee, and a loyalty mechanic, so it reads as more than a menu.
