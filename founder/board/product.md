# Product lens
Score: 5/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **There is a real hero product.** The five dessert-flavoured lattes (strawberry cheesecake matcha, cherry pie matcha, halva, apple strudel) and the hot chocolate with torched meringue are specific and easy to photograph. A torch flaring at the counter is a moment people will film. That gives the café one memorable thing to be known for, which most new coffee shops lack.
- **The constraint can work for it.** Morning only (6am–12pm), no permanent build-out and no utilities forces a small, focused operation. If it is used on purpose, "a pop-up café inside a bar" can be part of the story, not just something to hide.
- **The budget is low enough to learn cheaply.** At about $2,000, the founder can test whether the hero drinks pull customers before committing to anything bigger.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The space still feels like a bar at 7am.** Every customer touchpoint (smell, lighting, stools, the bar's own signage, whether there is a sign outside at all in the morning) belongs to someone else. The design can't be changed and the budget is $2,000, so the "funky, cute" experience may never show up. The Instagram visitor would arrive and find a closed-looking bar on Christopher St. If the first five minutes feel off, the hero drinks won't rescue it.
2. **No focus, so the morning line jams.** The menu has five signature lattes, a torched-meringue hot chocolate and a full regular coffee menu, all on a station of unknown capacity, possibly run by one person. A layered flavoured latte plus torching a meringue is slow. The weekday commuter wants a fast drip or espresso, and the weekend visitor wants a show. One person can't serve both well from the same bar in the same hour. The seam is at the hand-off from order to drink: if the wait gets long, commuters leave.
3. **Craft the customer can't see, at risk.** It isn't known whether the bar has a proper espresso machine, a grinder, a fridge or a dishwasher, or whether the founder may use them. Milk storage and dairy cleaning in a bar's kit, plus no confirmed health permit, is where quality and safety break first. One bad inspection or one bad-tasting batch, and the brand is gone.

## The questions I would ask the founder
1. Say it in one sentence: what is Hess Cafe, and why would someone walk to 70 Christopher St for it instead of the coffee shop they already pass?
2. Walk me through the first five minutes for a 7:30am weekday customer: what do they see from the street, where do they stand, how long until the drink is in their hand?
3. Which equipment does Mamali already have, can you use it, and what is your espresso and grinder setup if you can't?
4. Who works each shift? How many drinks an hour can that person make, with the signature drinks mixed in?
5. What does each signature drink cost to make, and what are the prices for the hot chocolate and the regular coffee?
6. How many customers a day do you expect, and why that number?
7. Can you put signage outside in the morning, and how do you make the room read as a café, not a closed bar, without changing the interior?
8. Do the bar's permits cover morning food service? Who is liable? What are the term and notice period of the deal?
9. With no food menu, what does a customer who wants something to eat do, and do you lose them?
10. If you could keep only two drinks, which would they be?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Cut the menu to the one thing.** At launch, offer two or three signature drinks (pick from data or a tasting) plus a minimal espresso and drip menu. Add drinks back only once peak-hour service time is proven.
2. **Show the first five minutes.** Before opening, show a concrete, removable plan for what the street and the room look like at 7am: outdoor signage permission, lighting, cups, music, and movable pieces, all within the budget.
3. **Confirm the parts nobody sees.** Get written confirmation of equipment access and condition, health/food service permit coverage, and liability, plus a signed agreement with a term and notice period.
4. **Prove the line.** Do a timed dry run of the full menu with the actual staffing, and set a target time from order to drink for the weekday rush.
5. **Know the unit cost.** Cost per drink for each item, and a daily-customer estimate with its reasoning, before the money is spent.
